TL;DR

"Right or wrong" is too coarse for free-form answers

A free-form answer can be right in many wordings. It can also be wrong in different ways: incomplete, padded with made-up facts, or flatly contradictory. Similarity metrics like BLEU, BERTScore and COMET mostly measure how much two texts look alike, so they can rate a contradiction almost perfectly:

The paper contributes three reusable pieces: an eight-class correctness taxonomy with an intended ordering; two datasets (CAP-Correctness, 8.8K labeled answers; CAP-Statements, 11K question-to-statement examples); and CAP (Context-Aware Precision), a metric that rewrites the gold and predicted answers as statements and checks entailment in both directions with an NLI model. Metrics are judged by whether their scores keep the taxonomy's order.

    How CAP scores an answer

    1. A fine-tuned mT5-small rewrites (question, gold) and (question, prediction) as declarative statements, sg and sp. 2. An NLI model (DeBERTa-v3-large) reads each statement pair in both directions and outputs entailment, neutral and contradiction probabilities. 3. Each direction gets a score: entailment plus a fraction λ of neutral. 4. The two directions are mixed with weight α.

    D(a → b) = Pent(a → b) + λ · Pneu(a → b)
    CAP = α · D(sg → sp) + (1 − α) · D(sp → sg)

    Paper setting: α = 0.85, λ = 0.30. Always in [0, 1].

    The key design trade-off

    Why α = 0.85 ranks partial above overinclusive-valid

    A partial answer ("water" for "solid water") is entailed by the gold, so the heavily weighted gold → prediction direction scores it high. An overinclusive-valid answer (the gold plus a true extra fact) is the mirror image: it entails the gold, but the gold doesn't entail the extras, so that direction mostly returns "neutral". Weighting gold → prediction at 0.85 therefore puts partial above overinclusive-valid, the opposite of the taxonomy's order. The chart shows CAP for both patterns as α varies:

    Does it work?

    Which metrics keep the order?

    Every metric scores all 7,827 test answers, and each measure checks agreement with the taxonomy's order. Lexical metrics and BERTScore are close to random. COMET does better. CAP roughly doubles COMET's rank correlation.

    Mean CAP per class: does it go down in order?

    A metric that respects the taxonomy should score each class, on average, below the classes above it. Rows are in taxonomy order, and any class that scores above a class ranked higher is flagged. The last three sources are real zero-shot answers from three LLMs (1,000 questions each), labeled by humans.

    Tables 5, 6 and 17. The violation counts are computed here from the class means and match the paper (4 synthetic, 4 human, 4 GPT-4o, 6 Gemini, 2 Qwen). For LLM answers, "n" is the number of answers in the class. Some classes are tiny (6 overinclusive-invalid answers for GPT-4o), so their means are noisy.

    Two failures repeat across sources. Alternative-correct is always too low: a valid different answer ("heat" instead of "electricity") neither entails nor is entailed by the gold, so NLI can't reward it without world knowledge. Partial tends to sit high, for the α reason above. The distribution itself is also informative: Gemini 2.0 Flash gives 251 partial answers out of 1,000, against 37 for GPT-4o.

    The hidden dependency

    Statement generation

    CAP only works if the statements are faithful. mT5-small is fine-tuned on CAP-Statements (8,800 / 1,100 / 1,100 split; triples generated with Claude Sonnet 4.6). On its test set it reaches 96.64 BLEU, 98.08 ROUGE-L and 76.7% exact match. In a manual check of 1,353 generated statements, 90.0% were correct. The errors cluster in one question type:

    Tables 10 and 15. Most "wrong" outputs just glue the question and answer together instead of rewriting them. You can see this in the dataset's own statements (e.g. "A hawk would prefer this for lunch A bison."). On long-context questions, those errors carry straight into the CAP score.

    Takeaway

    Grade how an answer relates to the gold, not how much it looks like it

    Checking entailment in both directions separates "says the same thing" from "says less" and "says more". A taxonomy with an explicit order turns metric evaluation into a checkable question: do scores go down as answers get worse? CAP answers yes more often than any baseline tested, but not always. Its remaining failures (alternative answers, partial vs. verbose) come from whole-statement NLI, and the authors suggest atomic-fact entailment as the next step. The benchmark and the monotonicity test can be reused for any future metric.
